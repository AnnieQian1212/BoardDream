# Built during the hackathon

All code was written after kickoff on Oct 26, 2026. The day-by-day plan and progress are in [PLAN.md](PLAN.md).

- **Oct 26:** engine, fuzzer, first reference game, search, app shell.
- **Oct 27:** 21 mechanic modules, three original reference games (Glasshouse, Lantern Isles, Highsail) and seven small test games, retrieval eval, 3D table, end-to-end game generation, playtesting with a strategic-depth check, Muse Spark opponents, card and board art.
- **Oct 28:** tile placement with edge matching, and Wildmere, a fourth original reference game built on it; card drafting, deck building and set collection modules; the generation benchmark rebuilt on hobby-game ideas; AI opponents on Muse Spark Open; a cooperative shared-threat module; Fenlight, a social deduction game where AI players argue and vote; game profiles measured by simulation; a results page; deployment setup; how-to-play cards and hints; print-and-play kits; rule changes in plain words.
- **Oct 29:** a night mode with dusk light, glowing lanterns and fog; water that ripples under the pointer; grass, flowers and trees on green cells.
- **Oct 30:** wheat on grain fields, rocks that glitter and chip on stony ground; a check that each painted tile shows its terrain; resource tokens that fall with Rapier physics; water, grass and stone on every game's ground; a Surprise me button that invents an idea and builds it.
- **Oct 31:** water as a glossy jelly: each pool domes above its tile with a rounded rim, bends the art beneath like a lens, and bounces when touched. Games are made about four times faster (low reasoning effort, measured on the benchmark; checks shared among worker threads), and a live view shows each step, with bots playing the draft on the table; closing the page stops the work.
- **Nov 1:** a storybook look: ink outlines on every piece, parchment boards, flat color, and the card and board art repainted to match. Generated maps name their places, show a number token on each (an elevation, a yield), mix their ground with water, and paint it by hand: grass and flowers, ferns, furrows, pebbles. A wider table: rivals stand in columns at the sides, the board is larger and the market stands upright beside it, and the side panels fold away. The table is drawn at the screen's own resolution, so text is sharp. Generated games can be kept or deleted, text stays inside its box, and the design stays up while a draft has a fault. In the evening, after an outside review: a home page that asks what you want to play, painted box covers, a full-screen table with the designer's tools in a drawer, a guide that says what to do each turn with the clickable spots pulsing, a layout for phones, and generated games that vary their board, mechanics and names instead of reaching for a hex map. Later that night: cards that set each other off (Highsail gained seven guild charters), gold flashes and floating changes on the table, flatter hand-drawn covers, four demo games instead of five, and the match and the turn moved out of the page into tested modules.
- **Nov 2:** covers in a warm, hand-painted film look, each framed its own way, set in the game's own place and peopled with players of every age and look; a new icon. The code review finished: one protocol for the design stream, a tested repair loop, one table vocabulary in the engine, the engine's own random playouts. Games are made at medium reasoning effort, which passes every benchmark idea within one repair. Look-alike moves fold into one button, and the flat table keeps the player's mat off the board. More ways to shape a game (board, how to win, tone, player powers, three new themes). Accounts and invite links: friends sign in, sit at your table and play your games with you, the server refereeing and bots in the empty seats. The demo went live at boarddream.pages.dev, deployed on every push. Light and dark themes (a lamp-lit evening table in the dark one), names that stay inside their tiles with values on hover, rivals seated evenly on both sides, a favorites star, and mochi friends to make at the foot of the page while a game is made, two alike pressed together into the next, from a dewdrop to the moon, and handed to woodland guests for coins that open tiles and buy upgrades. Through the evening: water with an even shore, islands raised above it and paths drawn across it; supplies that fit together as one block; the home page's question and idea box in one column; boards laid out lopsided sent back for a fix; wooden pieces cut as figures, houses, towers, ships, trees and robots, their numbers stamped in; chips sized where they stand on the table; the player picks how hard Muse Spark thinks, told the wait, and long thinking no longer drops the call; card art in a style picked for each game, each card's type on its corner; rules must place every piece and name every panel and card, and older games are made good where they are drawn. Late that night: wide screens no longer stretch the table's mats; a new game's whole deck is painted in the background; pieces and their labels drawn larger; names too small to read left to the hover tip; bare spaces printed with an inked frame and their emblem; switches to turn off the glow on clickable spots and to skip battles.
- **Nov 3:** water that looks alike whatever it is called: a wreck no longer ringed in gold beside the coves and reefs, a cove as full and clear as a reef. A market as wide as its column lays as many cards across as fit. The board painter now gets a guide colored by ground (every water a blue, every grassland a green) and the layout of its tiles; a painting that came back shifted is lined up with the board before it is cut into tiles, so no tile shows a band of gap along its top, and one laid out the painter's own way is painted again. Switches in the Art panel show or hide a game's card art and painted board. Every card gets a picture of its own, written for the whole deck at once, so a deck no longer repeats one figure on every card. The rules card explains a game in Chinese at a switch: its rules, how a turn goes, where its choices are, every card, and what the English words on the table mean.
